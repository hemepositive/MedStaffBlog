Sepsis remains the most common, deadliest, and one of the most costly inpatient diagnoses we treat. Patient outcomes depend on rapid recognition and early intervention.[^1]

<div class="key-message">

Sepsis is often treated before diagnostic certainty exists. Waiting for all criteria to be met may delay life-saving therapy — clinical judgment remains our most important diagnostic tool.[^1] [^2]

</div>

## Clinical expectation

Suspect sepsis early. At our organization, sepsis should be considered whenever a suspected or confirmed infection is accompanied by **new organ dysfunction**. A low threshold for initiating evaluation and treatment is encouraged when clinical concern exists.

**What counts as new organ dysfunction?** Under Sepsis-3, sepsis is life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection, operationalized as an acute increase in the SOFA score of ≥2 points attributable to infection (associated with in-hospital mortality >10%).[^3] At the bedside, watch for:

- Hypotension (SBP ≤90 mmHg, MAP ≤65 mmHg, or a drop ≥40 mmHg from baseline) or new vasopressor need
- Altered mental status
- New or worsening oliguria; rising creatinine
- Elevated lactate
- Rising bilirubin
- Hypoxemia (falling PaO₂/FiO₂); new increased oxygen requirement
- Thrombocytopenia or other new coagulopathy

qSOFA (≥2 of: RR ≥22, altered mentation, SBP ≤100 mmHg) can prompt escalation but is neither sensitive nor specific enough to rule sepsis in or out on its own.[^3]

## Evidence-based priorities

### Obtain blood cultures early

- Draw blood cultures in patients with suspected sepsis. The 2026 Surviving Sepsis Campaign (SSC) guideline gives a strong recommendation to collect blood cultures as soon as possible, ideally before antimicrobials, without delaying treatment.[^1]
- IDSA/ASM microbiology guidance similarly advises obtaining cultures before antibiotics whenever feasible.[^4]

### Administer antibiotics promptly (nuanced by severity and diagnostic certainty)

The 2026 SSC guideline ties antibiotic timing to both the presence of shock and the certainty of the sepsis diagnosis:[^1]

- **Within 1 hour** — for possible, probable, or definite **septic shock**, AND for **probable or definite sepsis without shock** (strong recommendations).
- **Within 3 hours** — for **possible sepsis without shock**, where an alternative (noninfectious) diagnosis remains plausible, following a rapid, time-limited assessment to clarify the diagnosis.[^1]

Choose empiric therapy appropriate to the patient's risk profile, reserving multidrug-resistant, antifungal, and anaerobic coverage for patients with appropriate risk factors.[^1]

### Use lactate to identify hypoperfusion

- Obtain a serum lactate early in the evaluation.[^1] [^5]
- A lactate >2 mmol/L should increase concern for tissue hypoperfusion and prompt reassessment of resuscitation; under Sepsis-3, lactate >2 mmol/L despite adequate fluids (with vasopressor-dependent hypotension) defines septic shock.[^3]
- Use serial lactate measurements to guide and reassess resuscitation.[^1]

### Provide guideline-based fluid resuscitation

For patients with sepsis-associated hypoperfusion, the 2026 SSC recommends **at least 30 mL/kg IV balanced crystalloid within the first 3 hours** (balanced solutions such as Lactated Ringer's are preferred over normal saline):[^1] [^6]

- Actual body weight if BMI <30
- Adjusted or ideal body weight if BMI ≥30

Treat 30 mL/kg as a starting target, not a ceiling or a fixed mandate. Continue frequent bedside reassessment (dynamic measures of fluid responsiveness, perfusion, and volume tolerance) to avoid both under- and over-resuscitation; harm signals emerge mainly at volumes far exceeding 30 mL/kg.[^1] [^6]

### Do not reflexively withhold 30 mL/kg for cardiac or kidney comorbidities

Reluctance to give the full weight-based volume in patients with heart failure, aortic stenosis, or kidney disease is common but is not well supported by the most recent data. In a 2026 Michigan Hospital Medicine Safety Consortium cohort of 25,481 adults with community-onset sepsis and an indication for fluid resuscitation (67 hospitals), administering ≥30 mL/kg vs <30 mL/kg within 6 hours was associated with lower adjusted 30-day mortality:[^7]

- **Hypoperfusion without severe comorbidities:** 26.0% vs 30.4% (adjusted absolute difference −4.4 percentage points).
- **Intermediate lactate (18–36 mg/dL) without severe comorbidities:** 12.0% vs 13.9% (−1.8 percentage points).
- **Hypoperfusion with severe cardiac or kidney comorbidities** (LVEF <40%, severe-to-critical aortic stenosis, or end-stage kidney disease): 34.7% vs 38.8% (−4.1 percentage points); this did not reach statistical significance, but spline models showed continuously decreasing mortality with higher fluid volumes in this subgroup.

Bottom line: comorbidities alone should not automatically trigger a reduced fluid target. Individualize with reassessment, but the default for hypoperfused patients — including many with cardiac or kidney disease — remains at least 30 mL/kg.[^7]

### Standardize care

Whenever clinically appropriate, utilize the Sepsis PowerPlan to streamline evidence-based management and improve bundle compliance.[^8]

## Documentation pearls

**CMS presumes sepsis unless documented otherwise**

- If acute organ dysfunction or clinical deterioration is determined to be unrelated to infection, clearly document the alternative diagnosis.[^8] [^9]

**When full fluid resuscitation is not appropriate**

- If you determine that the recommended fluid volume is not clinically indicated, briefly document the rationale (e.g., "concern for fluid overload"). Clinical judgment should always guide resuscitation decisions.[^9]

<div class="takeaway">

**Recognize early. Treat promptly. Reassess frequently. Document clearly.** Early intervention remains the single most effective strategy for improving outcomes in patients with sepsis.[^1] [^2]

</div>
