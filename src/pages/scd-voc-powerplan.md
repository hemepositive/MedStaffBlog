# Sickle Cell Disease Vaso-Occlusive Crisis

*Adult inpatient PowerPlan guide — consolidated reference for hospitalists, APPs, and pharmacy.*

<div class="key-message">

This guide consolidates institutional PowerPlan tip sheets and PCA instructions into a single quick reference for hospitalists, advanced practice providers, and pharmacy staff managing hospitalized adults in acute sickle cell vaso-occlusive crisis (VOC).

</div>

**Scope.** Adult inpatients admitted for uncomplicated acute VOC. This guide does not replace an existing Individualized Care Plan (ICP), pharmacy consultation, or clinical judgment.

## 1. Foundational Principles

- **Treat pain as an emergency.** Analgesia should be initiated within 60 minutes of arrival.
- **Check for an Individualized Care Plan (ICP) before ordering.** If one exists, it overrides standard weight-based dosing. The goal is to match — or exceed — the patient's home MME (morphine milligram equivalents) to overcome high baseline opioid tolerance.
- **Continue home medications unless contraindicated**, including:
  - *Hydroxyurea* — hold only if ANC < 2,000/mm³; obtain a CBC with differential on all patients.
  - *Deferasirox (Exjade/Jadenu)* — continue only using the patient's own home supply.
  - *Home oral opioid regimen* — continue in parallel with PCA per pharmacy guidance.
- **Fluid management: target euvolemia.** Give sufficient IV fluids but avoid overhydration — aggressive IVF can precipitate pulmonary edema and acute chest syndrome (ACS).

## 2. Multimodal, Non-Opioid Analgesia

Non-opioid analgesics should be **scheduled, not ordered PRN**, for the first 5–7 days of admission — most uncomplicated crises peak within 48–72 hours, and a steady state of anti-inflammatory therapy through resolution reduces total opioid requirement.

- Scheduled **acetaminophen 1 g every 8 hours**.
- Scheduled **ibuprofen 400–600 mg every 6–8 hours** (or naproxen), unless contraindicated by renal status or GI disease.
- **Adjuvants:** gabapentinoids or sub-anesthetic ketamine may be considered for pain refractory to standard PCA after 48 hours.

### NSAID selection: ibuprofen/naproxen preferred over ketorolac

Per ASH 2020 guidelines, ibuprofen and naproxen are preferred first-line NSAIDs based on a superior renal and gastrointestinal safety profile relative to ketorolac, without demonstrated superior opioid-sparing efficacy for ketorolac. Ketorolac may be considered only in select cases with adequate baseline renal function and close monitoring, and is time-limited (IV ≤ 2 days; oral ≤ 5 days).

### Renal function: assessment and monitoring

Almost all patients with SCD have some degree of baseline renal impairment from chronic hemolysis, anemia, and inflammation. Standard serum creatinine may underestimate true renal dysfunction in this population due to supranormal proximal tubular creatinine secretion — interpret creatinine and eGFR with this in mind, particularly in older adults.

- Assess baseline renal function (creatinine, eGFR, or creatinine clearance) before initiating scheduled NSAIDs.
- Avoid NSAIDs entirely if the patient has acute kidney injury on presentation.
- Ensure adequate hydration status before and during NSAID therapy.
- Monitor renal function during treatment; discontinue immediately if creatinine rises or urine output falls unexpectedly.
- Apply age-adjusted judgment — renal reserve declines with age.
- Limit ketorolac to the shortest duration possible (≤ 5 days); higher cumulative doses carry increased AKI risk.
- Involve nephrology/hematology for patients with pre-existing renal insufficiency or borderline cutoffs.

## 3. Opioid Analgesia via Patient-Controlled Analgesia (PCA)

PCA uses a computerized pump to deliver IV analgesia on the patient's own schedule. Compared with intermittent IV boluses, PCA shortens time-to-treatment; compared with continuous infusion, PCA achieves similar pain control with lower cumulative opioid exposure. Early, hospitalist-led PCA initiation has been associated with reduced length of stay.

**Prefer PCA over intermittent IV pushes.** Start with PCA, then transition to oral medication — do not "bridge" a patient who has already transitioned to oral meds with intermittent IV pushes. If pain becomes uncontrolled, restart the PCA instead.

### PCA components

- **Demand dose** — an opioid bolus delivered on patient request through a button, subject to a fixed lockout interval.
- **Lockout interval** — minimum time between demand doses; must be long enough for the prior dose to take effect.
- **Basal rate** — a continuous background infusion independent of demand dosing.

### Absolute contraindications to PCA

- Patient unable to understand the PCA concept.
- Decline in mental status with impending risk of airway compromise.
- Allergy to the PCA-delivered opioid.
- Infection at the PCA access site.

### Basal rates in adults

Routine use of basal continuous infusion is **not recommended** in adults and should be reserved for opioid-tolerant patients or those with a specific ICP indication. Basal rates increase the risk of nocturnal over-sedation and respiratory depression. Continuous pulse oximetry is **mandatory** for any patient on a basal rate, and recommended for the first 24 hours for all patients on PCA regardless of basal use.

Opioid tolerance is defined as **≥ 60 OME/day for ≥ 7 days**.

### Starting regimens — opioid-naïve (non-tolerant) adults

| Parameter | 1st line: HYDROmorphone | 2nd line: MORphine |
| --------- | ----------------------- | ------------------ |
| Demand dose | 0.4–1 mg (0.01–0.03 mg/kg) | 2 mg (0.02–0.03 mg/kg) |
| Lockout | 10 min | 10 min |
| 1-hour maximum | 4 mg | 10 mg |
| Starting basal rate | None, or 0.1–0.4 mg/hr | None, or 1–2 mg/hr |

### Starting regimens — opioid-tolerant adults

- Review the patient's home opioid regimen and the regimen used at prior hospitalizations.
- Consider initiating at the PCA dose used during the most recent prior hospitalization.
- PCA dosing is generally tiered by home OME: lower starting dose for < 60 OME/day, higher starting dose for > 60 OME/day.
- Engage pharmacy early for PCA initiation, titration, and the transition to oral pain medication.

Useful calculators: [MDCalc Morphine Milligram Equivalents](https://www.mdcalc.com/calc/10170/morphine-milligram-equivalents-mme-calculator) and [StatPearls, "Opioid Equivalency"](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK441913/). Note that hydromorphone and morphine potency differ substantially — 1 mg IV hydromorphone ≈ 6.6 mg IV morphine; 0.4 mg IV hydromorphone ≈ 2.6 mg IV morphine. **Confirm any conversion with pharmacy.**

## 4. Continuous Monitoring and Dose Titration

Review PCA use history and the nursing record regularly: number of attempted actuations (button presses, including those blocked by lockout), number of delivered doses, and total medication administered. Combine numeric pain scores with **functional** pain assessment — ability to cough, take a deep breath, and rest comfortably.

### Adverse effects to monitor

- **Over-sedation / respiratory depression** — order continuous end-tidal CO₂ monitoring if respiratory rate < 12.
- **Constipation** — scheduled stimulant laxative plus osmotic stool softener (pre-checked by default in the order set).
- Urinary retention, pruritus/itching, hypotension, insomnia, nausea/vomiting.

### If over-sedated or showing respiratory depression

- Hold all opioids until mental status improves.
- Consider naloxone.
- Optimize alternative non-opioid therapies (heat, acetaminophen, methocarbamol, temporary ibuprofen).
- If continued PCA use is required, lower bolus/basal rates and/or lengthen the lockout interval.

### If pain relief is inadequate

- **Repeated end-of-lockout activations with otherwise adequate relief** → shorten the lockout interval (e.g., 10 min → 8 min).
- **Consistent repeated actuations without relief** → escalate the demand dose by 50% for somewhat unrelieved pain, or 100% for completely uncontrolled pain (e.g., hydromorphone 0.4 mg → 0.6 mg).
- **No relief despite significant dose/interval adjustment** → optimize non-opioid adjuncts, rotate to a different opioid at an equipotent dose, and evaluate for an alternative pain etiology (e.g., infection, compartment syndrome).

## 5. Supportive Care

- **Antiemetics/antipruritics:** favor oral formulations (e.g., oral ondansetron ODT) when the patient is tolerating PO, to support progression toward discharge readiness. Where an IV antihistamine such as diphenhydramine is being considered, weigh its additive sedating effect on top of opioid therapy — that compounded sedation risk is the reason to prefer non-sedating or oral alternatives when clinically appropriate.
- **Incentive spirometry:** order 10 breaths every hour while awake. This is a key, low-risk intervention to prevent acute chest syndrome in patients with rib or back pain who may be under-ventilating due to pain or sedation.
- **Use heat, not ice:** cold application can cause local vasoconstriction and worsen sickling and pain; heat therapy is preferred.

## 6. Red Blood Cell Transfusion — When (and When Not) to Transfuse

<div class="key-message">

**Do not transfuse patients with an uncomplicated acute pain crisis.** Trial data show transfusion within 24 hours of admission for pain alone does not improve length of stay, opioid requirement, or pain scores. Transfuse only when an additional clinical indication exists beyond the pain crisis itself.

</div>

### Indications beyond uncomplicated pain

- Acute ischemic stroke
- Acute chest syndrome — rapidly progressive or severe
- Multi-organ failure
- Fat embolism syndrome
- Acute anemia — hemoglobin drop > 2 g/dL below the patient's baseline
- Splenic sequestration crisis (transfuse cautiously — risk of rebound hemoglobin rise as sequestered cells release)

### Simple vs. exchange transfusion

| | Simple transfusion | Exchange transfusion (preferred for severe cases) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mechanism | Adds packed RBCs; does not remove HbS | Removes sickle RBCs, replaces with donor RBCs (apheresis or manual) |
| Effect on HbS% | No reduction | Reduces HbS by ~30% |
| Access | Peripheral IV | Central access required |
| Best for | Rapid, simple correction | Severe ACS, stroke, multi-organ failure |

### Critical safety parameters

- **Never exceed a target hemoglobin of 10 g/dL** (hematocrit 30%) in acute transfusion — exceeding this raises the risk of hyperviscosity, VOC, stroke, and venous thromboembolism.
- Post-transfusion hematocrit should not exceed 36%.
- Do not transfuse patients with a high baseline Hb (> 10 g/dL) absent another clinical indication.

## 7. Transitioning From PCA to Oral Opioids

- Discuss the plan to transition to oral opioids with the patient **early**, before it happens.
- **24-hour rule:** do not transition to oral medication until the patient has had stable pain control with minimal demand-dose activations for 24 hours.
- If a basal rate is running, decrease or stop it, but continue bolus (demand) dosing for 24 hours.
- Assess total opioid equivalents used over the prior 24 hours and convert to an oral opioid–equivalent dose (pharmacy can assist).
- Schedule the oral opioid regimen once the basal rate is stopped:
  - *Non-opioid-tolerant patients* — consider transitioning to a home opioid regimen.
  - *Opioid-tolerant patients* — continue the home regimen with adjustments as needed.
- Monitor for opioid withdrawal (sweating, diarrhea, agitation, tachycardia, nasal congestion, piloerection) and adjust dosing if it occurs.
- Do not bridge with intermittent IV pushes once a patient is on oral medication — return to PCA if pain becomes uncontrolled.

## 8. Individualize and Document the Care Plan

Vaso-occlusive crisis management differs for every patient with sickle cell disease.

- Document the pain plan in the current hospitalization **and** in the discharge summary to support optimal pain control, shorter length of stay, and patient satisfaction.
- A clearly documented pain management course helps colleagues manage the same patient during a future crisis.

## Quick-Reference Summary

| Domain | Recommendation |
| ------ | -------------- |
| First dose | Within 60 min of arrival; use the ICP if one exists. |
| Non-opioid analgesia | Scheduled acetaminophen + ibuprofen/naproxen for 5–7 days (not PRN); check renal function first. |
| Opioid route | PCA preferred; avoid intermittent IV pushes. Basal rate only if opioid-tolerant or ICP-directed. |
| PCA start (naïve) | Hydromorphone 0.4–1 mg / 10-min lockout (1st line); morphine 2 mg / 10-min lockout (2nd line). |
| Monitoring | Continuous pulse oximetry (mandatory with basal rate); end-tidal CO₂ if RR < 12; scheduled bowel regimen. |
| Transfusion | Not for uncomplicated pain crisis; reserve for stroke, severe ACS, multi-organ failure, fat embolism, Hb drop > 2 g/dL, or sequestration. |
| ACS prevention | Incentive spirometry, 10 breaths/hour while awake; heat therapy, not ice. |
| Transition to oral | After 24 h of stable control with minimal demand doses; wean basal first, continue bolus 24 h; monitor for withdrawal. |

<div class="takeaway">

Treat the pain within an hour, follow the ICP if there is one, schedule non-opioid analgesia rather than ordering it PRN, start with PCA instead of IV pushes, and reserve transfusion for complications beyond the pain crisis itself.

</div>
